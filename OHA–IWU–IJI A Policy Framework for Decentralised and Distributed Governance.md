# OHA–IWU–IJI

## A Policy Framework for Decentralised and Distributed Governance

**Status:** Draft policy paper for consultation
**Author:** Nnamdi Michael Okpala
**Date:** 23 July 2026

---

## Executive Summary

This policy paper presents **OHA–IWU–IJI** as an architectural philosophy for governance.

Its central argument is that governance should not be understood merely as the writing and enforcement of laws. Governance must be deliberately designed as a decentralised and distributed system through which people, communities and states receive the authority and resources needed to govern themselves.

Under this framework, the first duty of government is to:

> **Decentralise power, distribute resources and enable communities to build the conditions required to protect life and uphold fair law.**

The framework rejects excessive dependence on a single national leader or central authority. It proposes that decision-making, public resources and administrative responsibility should be distributed among states and local communities.

It further proposes a universal material baseline, potentially including a form of universal income, so that people are not forced into preventable suffering before the state demands compliance, work or productivity.

Governance, in this model, is architectural. It must be visible, understandable and capable of being physically represented. A system that cannot be explained clearly to the people it governs risks becoming inaccessible, exclusionary and fragile. 

---

## 1. Policy Context

Many governance systems concentrate political authority, public finance and institutional decision-making within a small central structure.

This can create several problems:

* local communities may lack meaningful control over their own development;
* public resources may not reach the people who need them;
* policies may work for one demographic while excluding another;
* citizens may be required to obey systems they had little role in designing;
* institutional failure affecting one person may later be repeated against many others.

The OHA–IWU–IJI framework begins from the position that failure is not always personal. Failure may be architectural.

When a governance system repeatedly excludes or harms individuals, the problem may lie in how the system is designed rather than in the people attempting to use it.

The policy response is therefore not limited to replacing leaders or writing additional rules. It requires redesigning how power, resources, protection and accountability move through society.

---

## 2. Definition of Governance

Within this framework, governance is defined as:

> **The architecture through which public power, resources, protection, law and democratic responsibility are distributed among people and communities.**

Governance is not a switch controlled by one officeholder.

It is not merely a collection of documents, policies or commands.

It is a designed system of relationships between:

* individuals;
* communities;
* local institutions;
* states or regions;
* national authorities; and
* systems of public accountability.

The effectiveness of governance depends on whether these relationships enable people to protect life, participate in decisions and build their own communities.

## 3. The OHA–IWU–IJI Framework

### 3.1 OHA — Community and Public Enablement

In the source framework, **OHA** refers to the community or public.

Its role is to ensure that governance begins with people rather than with institutions.

OHA should provide communities with:

* access to public resources;
* meaningful local decision-making;
* the ability to identify their own priorities;
* mechanisms for public participation;
* protection from arbitrary central control; and
* the practical ability to build local infrastructure.

OHA represents the public foundation of governance.

A government cannot credibly claim to empower people while withholding the resources and authority required for them to act.

### 3.2 IWU — Law and Policy

**IWU** concerns law and policy.

Under this framework, law should support public capability rather than merely impose obligations.

Policies should not be designed only for people who already understand complex institutions, possess financial security or fit a preferred administrative category.

Law must be capable of serving different communities and demographics.

This requires policy systems that are:

* understandable;
* accessible;
* adaptable;
* publicly accountable; and
* connected to lived conditions.

Strict laws that operate effectively for only one category of people should be reviewed as design failures.

The purpose of law is not simply to produce compliance. It is to establish a fair and stable baseline through which communities can function.

### 3.3 IJI — Order and Practical Governance

**IJI** concerns the organisation and practical execution of governance.

It connects public participation and legal policy to real implementation.

IJI should determine:

* how resources are allocated;
* which level of government is responsible for each service;
* how decisions are reviewed;
* how corruption is detected;
* how institutional failures are corrected; and
* how citizens can challenge decisions.

Under IJI, authority should be distributed rather than accumulated indefinitely at the centre.

Public order should emerge from accountable design, not from the assumption that one leader can personally supervise an entire society—a charming fantasy humans revisit every few decades.

---

## 4. Primary Policy Objective

The primary objective of OHA–IWU–IJI is:

> **To give people and communities sufficient political authority, material resources and institutional capacity to govern themselves within an accountable democratic structure.**

This objective does not eliminate national government.

Instead, it changes the role of central government.

The centre should:

* establish constitutional protections;
* coordinate issues that cross regional boundaries;
* maintain common standards;
* distribute resources fairly;
* prevent abuse by local or regional authorities; and
* ensure that decentralisation does not become abandonment.

Local and regional institutions should have the authority to design and deliver policies suited to their communities, provided that they respect constitutional rights and public accountability.

---

## 5. Decentralisation of Power

The framework proposes that political and administrative power should be distributed across multiple levels of government.

This may include:

* community institutions;
* local councils;
* municipalities;
* states or regions; and
* national government.

Each level should have clearly defined responsibilities.

Decentralisation should not mean that the central government simply transfers duties while retaining the money. That familiar bureaucratic magic trick produces responsibility without capability.

A genuine decentralisation policy must transfer:

1. decision-making authority;
2. adequate funding;
3. administrative capacity;
4. access to relevant information; and
5. responsibility for measurable outcomes.

The principle is:

> **Authority and resources must travel together.**

---

## 6. Distribution of Public Resources

The source framework places resource distribution before the full enforcement of state obligations.

This reflects the argument that people require a minimum level of material security before government can reasonably demand productivity or compliance.

Public resources should therefore be distributed to ensure access to essential conditions of life.

These may include:

* food;
* clean water;
* housing;
* healthcare;
* education;
* energy;
* transportation;
* digital access; and
* basic income security.

The transcript specifically proposes a form of **universal income** or **universal basic income**, although the precise terminology and policy design remain unresolved. 

Further work would be required to define:

* eligibility;
* payment level;
* funding;
* interaction with employment;
* interaction with disability and housing support;
* regional cost differences; and
* safeguards against inflation or administrative exclusion.

The underlying policy principle is nevertheless clear:

> No person should be left without the resources necessary to avoid preventable suffering.

---

## 7. Protection Before Enforcement

Under OHA–IWU–IJI, enforcement should follow the establishment of a fair baseline.

This does not mean that all law enforcement should stop until every social problem is resolved.

It means that government must examine whether its own failure has contributed to the conduct it seeks to punish.

Before enforcing a rule, public authorities should consider:

* whether the person understood the requirement;
* whether the requirement was accessible;
* whether the person possessed the resources needed to comply;
* whether disability or neurodivergence affected participation;
* whether the policy worked equitably across demographics; and
* whether institutional neglect created the problem.

This establishes a distinction between:

* deliberate harmful conduct; and
* conduct arising from deprivation, exclusion or inaccessible systems.

The policy objective is not the removal of responsibility. It is the fair placement of responsibility.

## 8. Governance as Visible Architecture

A distinctive part of this framework is the claim that governance should be capable of being **drawn physically**.

The transcript contrasts geometric representation with abstract algebraic constructs, arguing that overly abstract systems may become fragile at governance scale. 

In policy terms, this can be developed into a requirement that governance structures be visually explainable.

Every major institution should publish a clear representation showing:

* where authority begins;
* where public money moves;
* who makes each decision;
* who reviews that decision;
* how citizens participate;
* how complaints are escalated; and
* how officials are removed or replaced.

A governance diagram might show:

```text
PEOPLE AND COMMUNITIES
          ↓
         OHA
Public participation and community priorities
          ↓
         IWU
Law, policy and constitutional safeguards
          ↓
         IJI
Implementation, resource distribution and review
          ↓
PUBLIC OUTCOMES
          ↓
Community feedback and democratic correction
          ↺
```

This visible architecture would allow citizens to identify where a system has failed.

A person should not require advanced legal training merely to discover which office lost their application.

## 9. Anti-Corruption Design

The transcript identifies corruption as a problem that should be addressed through system design rather than relying solely on individual morality.

The framework therefore requires anti-corruption mechanisms at every level of governance.

These should include:

* transparent budgets;
* published procurement decisions;
* independent auditing;
* declarations of financial interests;
* public registers of government contracts;
* clear conflict-of-interest rules;
* protection for whistleblowers;
* traceable transfers between national and regional authorities; and
* public access to non-sensitive institutional records.

Decentralisation must also include safeguards against local corruption.

Moving corruption from a national office to several regional offices would be distribution, technically, but not the inspiring kind.

Each decentralised authority should therefore remain subject to:

* constitutional review;
* independent courts;
* public audit;
* electoral accountability; and
* enforceable human-rights protections.

---

## 10. Democratic Leadership

The framework supports leadership through public choice.

A person may propose to lead, but leadership must be obtained through a legitimate vote rather than through personal declaration.

Democratic leadership should require:

* open elections;
* equal eligibility rules;
* transparent campaign finance;
* independent election administration;
* peaceful transfers of power;
* fixed or limited terms of office; and
* procedures for recall, impeachment or removal.

The role of a leader is not to own the governance architecture.

The leader operates within it.

No leader should possess the power to override the constitutional distribution of authority merely because they believe they understand the needs of the people.

## 11. Implementation Model

The OHA–IWU–IJI framework could be developed through five phases.

### Phase One — Public Definition

Establish clear public definitions of:

* OHA;
* IWU;
* IJI;
* decentralisation;
* distributed governance;
* universal baseline;
* local autonomy; and
* constitutional accountability.

### Phase Two — Governance Mapping

Create diagrams of existing governance systems showing:

* institutions;
* financial flows;
* decision-making authority;
* appeals;
* failures; and
* areas of duplication.

### Phase Three — Pilot Communities

Test the framework in selected communities or public programmes.

Pilots could examine whether local control improves:

* housing delivery;
* public infrastructure;
* social support;
* disability access;
* education;
* water provision; or
* community budgeting.

### Phase Four — Legal and Financial Allocation

Define which powers and funding should belong to:

* communities;
* local government;
* regional government; and
* national government.

### Phase Five — Review and Expansion

Measure outcomes, publish failures and revise the architecture before wider implementation.

---

## 12. Evaluation Principles

The framework should be evaluated using practical public outcomes.

Possible indicators include:

* access to essential services;
* percentage of public funding reaching local communities;
* time required to resolve complaints;
* levels of public participation;
* regional inequality;
* corruption findings;
* housing and infrastructure delivery;
* accessibility for disabled and neurodivergent citizens; and
* public understanding of government responsibilities.

The central evaluation question should be:

> **Does this system give people greater practical power to protect life and build their communities?**

A policy should not be considered successful merely because it was enacted.

Its success depends on whether people can use it.

---

## 13. Unresolved Policy Questions

The source establishes the philosophy of the framework but does not yet define every operational mechanism.

Further policy development is needed concerning:

* the exact constitutional relationship between OHA, IWU and IJI;
* the meaning and funding of universal income;
* which resources should be controlled locally;
* how national standards would be maintained;
* how regional corruption would be prevented;
* how geometric governance models would be formalised;
* how disputes between levels of government would be resolved; and
* how the framework would apply during emergencies.

These gaps do not invalidate the framework. They identify the next stage of work: turning a philosophy into testable institutional design.

---

## Conclusion

OHA–IWU–IJI proposes that governance should be designed from the public outward rather than imposed from the centre downward.

Its central principles are:

> **Distribute power.
> Distribute resources.
> Establish a universal baseline.
> Protect life.
> Apply fair law.
> Enable people to build their own communities.**

The framework treats governance as architecture: something that can be examined, drawn, tested, repaired and improved.

Democracy, under this model, is not limited to voting for a national leader.

It means giving people the authority, resources and institutional protection required to participate in governing their own lives.

A government succeeds not when it controls every decision, but when its people possess the capacity to make sound decisions without being abandoned, excluded or dominated.
